What Is TDEE?

⚡ Quick Definition: TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) is the total number of kilocalories your body burns in 24 hours. It is calculated by multiplying your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) by your Physical Activity Level (PAL) factor:

TDEE = BMR × Activity Factor

TDEE stands for Total Daily Energy Expenditure. It is an estimate of the total energy, expressed in kilocalories (kcal), that your body burns over a 24-hour period — including energy used at rest, during physical activity, and to digest food.

Your daily energy expenditure is made up of four components:

  • BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate): Energy used at rest to keep essential body functions running — breathing, circulation, brain activity. This accounts for 60%–70% of total daily burn. (NCBI [2])
  • NEAT (Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis): Energy used for everyday movement — walking, standing, household chores, commuting. NEAT can vary by 800+ kcal/day between individuals.
  • TEF (Thermic Effect of Food): Energy required to digest and process food (roughly 10% of daily intake). Protein has the highest thermic cost (20%–30%).
  • EAT (Exercise Activity Thermogenesis): Energy burned during planned workouts — running, strength training, cycling, and sports.

Standard TDEE Activity Level Multipliers

Physical Activity Level (PAL) multipliers adjust your resting metabolic floor to account for non-exercise movement (NEAT) and structured exercise (EAT):

Activity Level Multiplier Weekly Exercise Routine Daily Step Equivalent
Sedentary 1.200 Little to no structured exercise, desk job Under 5,000 steps/day
Lightly Active 1.375 Light exercise or sports 1–3 days/week 5,000 – 8,000 steps/day
Moderately Active 1.550 Moderate exercise or gym sessions 3–5 days/week 8,000 – 12,000 steps/day
Very Active 1.725 Hard training or heavy lifting 6–7 days/week 12,000 – 15,000 steps/day
Extra Active 1.900 Physical job (construction, nurse) + daily intense workouts 15,000+ steps/day

Calorie Goals: Maintenance, Deficit & Surplus Targets

Once you calculate your TDEE, apply evidence-based calorie targets tailored to your specific fitness objective:

Goal Strategy Calorie Adjustment Expected Rate of Change Recommended For
Maintenance 100% of TDEE (±0 kcal) Weight stable Weight retention, athletic performance, diet breaks
Mild Deficit 85% of TDEE (−15%) −0.25 kg (~0.5 lb) / week Lean individuals, maximum muscle & strength retention
Optimal Deficit 78% of TDEE (−22%) −0.50 kg (~1.1 lb) / week Standard sustainable fat loss for most healthy adults
Aggressive Deficit 70% of TDEE (−30%) −0.80 kg (~1.75 lb) / week Higher body fat levels or short-term rapid fat loss
Lean Muscle Surplus 110% of TDEE (+10%) +0.20 kg (~0.4 lb) / week Resistance trained lifters maximizing hypertrophy with minimal fat

Worked Clinical Math Example

Example Profile: 30-year-old male, 80 kg (176 lbs), 180 cm (5 ft 11 in), moderately active (lifts weights 4 days/week).

Step 1: Calculate BMR (Mifflin-St Jeor)
BMR = (10 × 80) + (6.25 × 180) − (5 × 30) + 5 = 800 + 1,125 − 150 + 5 = 1,780 kcal/day
Step 2: Apply Activity Multiplier
TDEE = 1,780 × 1.55 (Moderately Active) = 2,759 kcal/day
Step 3: Calculate Fat Loss Calorie Target (-22% Optimal Deficit)
Target Calories = 2,759 × 0.78 = 2,152 kcal/day (~0.5 kg loss/week)

Frequently Asked Questions

What does TDEE stand for? ▼

TDEE stands for Total Daily Energy Expenditure. It is an estimate of the total energy your body uses during a 24-hour period.

What is a TDEE calculator? ▼

A TDEE calculator estimates your daily energy expenditure using information such as age, sex, height, weight, and activity level.

How is TDEE calculated? ▼

A common method estimates resting energy expenditure and then multiplies it by an activity factor.

Estimated TDEE = Estimated REE × Activity Factor

What formula does this TDEE calculator use? ▼

The calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation to estimate resting energy expenditure and then applies an activity factor. The original Mifflin-St Jeor study was published in 1990. (PubMed [1])

Is Mifflin-St Jeor accurate? ▼

Mifflin-St Jeor is a widely used predictive equation and has performed well compared with several older equations. However, it is still a prediction and cannot be exact for every individual.

Is TDEE the same as maintenance calories? ▼

Not exactly.

TDEE is an estimate of energy expenditure. Maintenance calories describe an intake level associated with maintaining body weight over time.

TDEE can be used as a starting estimate for maintenance calories.

How accurate is a TDEE calculator? ▼

A TDEE calculator provides an estimate rather than a direct measurement.

Your actual energy needs can differ because activity, body composition, daily movement, and other factors vary between people.

Why do two TDEE calculators give different results? ▼

They may use different equations, activity factors, assumptions, or calculation methods.

Which activity level should I choose? ▼

Choose the level that best represents your normal overall activity, including exercise, work, walking, and other daily movement.

Can I use TDEE for weight loss? ▼

Yes. Your estimated TDEE can provide a starting point for understanding your maintenance calories and planning a calorie deficit.

Can I use TDEE for weight gain? ▼

Yes. Your estimated TDEE can provide a starting point for planning a calorie surplus.

Does TDEE change when I lose weight? ▼

It can. Changes in body weight and activity can change estimated energy needs.

Should I recalculate TDEE every day? ▼

No. Daily recalculation is usually unnecessary. Recalculate when your body weight, activity, or routine changes meaningfully.

Can TDEE be measured? ▼

Yes, energy expenditure can be measured using scientific methods such as indirect calorimetry and doubly labeled water. A normal online TDEE calculator estimates rather than measures your TDEE. (PubMed [5])

Should men and women calculate TDEE differently? ▼

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation uses different constants for men and women, so sex is one of its inputs. (PubMed [1])

What if my calculated TDEE seems too high or too low? ▼

Check your input information and activity level first. Then compare the estimate with your calorie intake and body-weight trend over several weeks.
